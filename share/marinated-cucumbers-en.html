Marinated Cucumbers

Ingredients

Brine quantity is calculated for 1 liter of potable water.

Ingredient Amount
Cucumbers To fill the jar
Potable water 1 L
Table vinegar 9% 5 tbsp
Salt 36 g
Sugar 42 g
Garlic 2–3 cloves
Fresh dill 2 sprigs
Fresh chili pepper 10 g

The amounts of salt and sugar correspond to the heaping tablespoons used in the source recipe: roughly 18 g of salt and 14 g of sugar respectively. Mass may vary with different salt, sugar, or utensils, so weighing is preferred.

Choosing Salt

No specific salt type is mandated by the recipe. When selecting salt, consider its composition, grain size, and manufacturer recommendations for pickling and preserving.

Iodine and anti-caking agents can affect brine color and clarity. For accurate proportions, weigh the salt rather than relying solely on spoon volume.

A 3-liter jar typically takes about 1.2–1.5 L of brine. Actual volume depends on cucumber size and packing density.

Preparation

1. Cucumber Preparation

Wash the cucumbers and place them in a suitable vessel. Pour boiling water over them so all cucumbers are completely submerged.

Cover with a lid, wrap in cloth on top, and leave until the water cools completely to room temperature.

Cooling time depends on vessel volume and ambient conditions. For a 3-liter jar, it can reach 6 hours.

During cooling, cucumbers may darken toward a brownish hue. However, color change is not a required condition or completion criterion for this step—water temperature is the primary indicator.

Once completely cooled, drain the water.

2. Jar and Ingredient Preparation

Prepare and treat the jars and lids.

This practice uses steam treatment for jars: water is poured into a pot, a metal conical sieve is placed on top, and the jar is set upside down over it. To speed up heating, the outside of the jar is additionally poured with boiling water.

In this practice, treatment lasts 10–15 minutes of active boiling after the jar is fully heated through.

Place lids in a separate container and pour boiling water over them. Use kitchen tongs to retrieve and place them.

Put garlic, dill, and chili pepper in a separate container and briefly scald with boiling water. Avoid overholding delicate ingredients—especially dill—to limit prolonged exposure to high heat.

3. Brine Preparation

Pour the required amount of potable water into a pot.

Bring to a boil, add salt and sugar, and boil for one minute.

Then add 9% table vinegar.

4. Packing the Jar

Place the scalded garlic, dill, and chili pepper on the bottom of the prepared jar.

Then pack in the cucumbers. Arrange them densely, without ramming or compressing.

It is helpful to estimate cucumber volume beforehand by test-packing raw cucumbers into the jar.

Tomato option. If there are not enough cucumbers to fill the jar, open space can be filled with fresh tomatoes. This option is provided in the practical version of the recipe and allows using available cucumbers without extra prep.

Wash the tomatoes and scald with boiling water. The preliminary preparation used for cucumbers is not required for tomatoes.

5. Filling and Sealing

Pour boiling brine over the packed ingredients.

In this practice, brine is filled almost to the very rim of the neck. Some liquid may spill when placing the lid.

Put on the treated lid and seal the jar with a sterilized canning key.

Invert the jar and visually check for brine leakage or visible bubbles escaping from under the lid.

Wrap the jar in cloth and leave until completely cool. Depending on conditions, this takes around 12 hours; leaving it overnight is convenient.

6. Readiness

In this practice, tasting begins starting from the third week after preparation.

Brine Volume

A 3-liter jar usually requires 1.2–1.5 liters of brine.

Differences stem from cucumber size, inclusion of tomatoes, and packing density.

It is best to prepare brine with a small margin.

Practical Observations

Based on practical preparation and storage experience, the following has been noted:

  • Individual jars were stored in a refrigerator for about six months.
  • A one-year benchmark is not based on exact observations and is not a verified safe storage period.
  • Once opened, contents are typically used within a week. This is personal practice rather than an established shelf life.
  • Do not consume the product if slime, signs of fermentation, or other changes appear.

Suitable conditions and timelines for storage in a cellar or other room have not been established.

Note on the Source Recipe

The original version by Tatyana Zhuravskaya also mentioned replacing vinegar with citric acid: slightly less than one teaspoon directly in the jar.

This substitution was not tested in practical experience, so it cannot be considered a verified equivalent to vinegar or a recommended recipe modification.
