How ordinary orchard and forest leaves transform through natural fermentation into a deep, aromatic, amber-colored infusion.

Organization Dmitry Lab Document ID DL-BOT-2024-01
Category Botanical & Culinary Craft Version / Date v1.2 · 2024-01-10

Essence of the Process: Natural fermentation activates the plant's own enzymes and beneficial surface microflora. Under controlled moisture and warmth, astringent green compounds break down and give way to rich fruity, floral, and honey notes—without any artificial additives.

Choosing the Right Plants

Not every garden leaf yields a balanced cup. Two natural criteria determine whether a plant is suitable for craft tea making:

  • Tannin content: Tannins give tea its body, color, and noble astringency. Young, tender spring and early-summer leaves hold the highest concentration; as leaves coarsen toward autumn, tannin levels drop sharply.
  • Edible fruits or berries: A reliable rule of thumb is to choose trees and shrubs bearing edible fruit—apple, pear, cherry, plum, blackthorn, serviceberry, blackcurrant, raspberry, strawberry, chokeberry, grape, quince, and oleaster.

Harvesting and Sorting

Gather leaves in dry weather, favoring plants growing in partial shade where foliage stays juicier and more supple. Avoid washing the leaves before processing—water washes away the natural wild microflora required to kick-start fermentation. Rinse only visibly dusty or soiled leaves and let them air-dry completely.

SPRING

Spring

Leaves are delicate, pliable, easy to roll, and ferment quickly into a light, fragrant infusion.

SUMMER

Summer

Mature leaves—especially slightly reddened ones—accumulate peak organic acids and aromatic compounds.

AUTUMN

Autumn

Foliage turns dense and fibrous; hard leaves from orchard trees require firmer rolling and longer fermentation.

Before withering, sort through the harvest and remove overly dry, sun-scorched, or insect-damaged leaves.

Withering

Withering gently reduces moisture so the leaf becomes soft and leathery rather than brittle. Depending on indoor humidity and leaf toughness, choose one of two methods:

METHOD A · CLASSIC

Classic Method (On Cloth)

Spread leaves indoors in a 3–5 cm layer on clean cotton or linen fabric. Toss gently every few hours for even drying. Keep out of direct sunlight and wind, which dry the edges too fast.

METHOD B · ACCELERATED

Accelerated Method (In a Bag)

Ideal for dry rooms or tough leaves. Tie leaves inside a cotton or linen sack, then twist, knead, and shake periodically. The cloth shields against drafts and yields a darker, pu-erh-like profile.

Process Parameters: Temperature 20–24 °C · Humidity ~70% · Duration ~12 hours.

Readiness signs: Fold a leaf in half—the central vein no longer snaps. Squeeze a handful tightly in your fist—the green clump holds its shape without springing open.

Important Restriction

Do not leave withered leaves piled up for more than 1–2 days; proceed directly to cell disruption so the batch does not spoil or dry out unevenly.

Freezing (Optional Step)

Placing withered leaves in the freezer makes subsequent rolling noticeably easier. Ice crystals rupture cell walls from within, releasing juice rapidly once thawed. Prepared leaves keep well in a freezer for up to 2 months.

Disrupting Leaf Structure

To launch oxidation, the leaf's cellular structure must be broken until sap appears on the surface. The technique you choose defines the final grade of tea:

GRADE · FINE-LEAF

Hand Rolling

Roll 7–10 leaves firmly between your palms until they darken with sap, forming tight rolls 10 cm long and 1–1.5 cm thick. Slice before drying for fine-leaf tea.

GRADE · LOOSE-LEAF

Kneading in a Bowl

Knead leaves vigorously in a deep, wide bowl for 15–20 min, breaking up clumps and fluffing stuck leaves until sap coats the surface. Produces classic loose-leaf tea.

GRADE · GRANULATED

Meat Grinder

Pass withered leaves through a grinder fitted with a coarse plate. Yields dense, fast-brewing granulated tea with a rich, dark liquor.

Fermentation

Pack the bruised leaves into a clean glass, ceramic, or food-grade enamel vessel. Place hand-rolled and bowl-kneaded leaves under a light weight; press granulated leaves down firmly by hand.

Cover the container with a damp cotton or linen cloth (re-moistening it as it dries) and move it to a warm spot. In dry air, place a lid loosely on top while leaving a small gap for airflow.

Fermentation Window: Optimal range 22–26 °C · Typical duration 6–8 hours.

Below 15 °C, fermentation stalls. Above 30 °C, soluble flavor compounds turn insoluble and delicate aromas burn off. Stop fermentation the moment a vivid fruity-floral bouquet peaks—if left too long, the fragrance fades into a flat, musty note.

Drying and Curing

If using hand-rolled cigars, slice them into 0.5 cm rings (leaving them whole produces a lighter-colored loose-leaf infusion). Spread the tea in a 1 cm layer on a parchment-lined baking tray.

  • Oven drying: Bake at 100 °C for 1–1.5 hours, stirring regularly. (Traditional Chinese makers start at 125–150 °C for 10–20 min to caramelize surface sugars and seal juices inside the granules.) Lower the heat to 50–60 °C until leaves turn crisp without scorching.
  • Air curing: Transfer cooled tea into thin cotton or linen bags and hang in a dry, ventilated room or shaded outdoor spot for at least a week to equalize residual moisture.

Readiness signs: Properly dried leaves snap cleanly when pressed rather than bending or crumbling into dust, and produce a dry rustling sound when stirred.

Storage and Maturation

Store finished tea in airtight glass jars or food-grade containers in a dark, dry cupboard. Subtle dry maturation continues during storage, rounding out rough edges and deepening the bouquet. The tea reaches its best flavor after resting for at least 1 month.
