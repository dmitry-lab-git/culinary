Introduction

Fermentation is the biochemical transformation of organic substances through the action of enzymes or microorganisms.

An enzyme (from the Latin fermentum, meaning "leaven" or "ferment") is generally a complex protein compound, RNA molecule (ribozyme), or a combination of these that accelerates chemical reactions in living systems.

Process characteristics in leaf processing. In the context of preparing herbal infusions, the term fermentation is used below primarily to refer to the enzymatic oxidation of substances contained in plant material. When the cellular structure of a leaf is damaged, enzymes interact with the substances present within it. This results in changes to the colour, taste, and aroma of the final product.

Basic Principles for Selecting Plants

  • 1. The presence of tannins in the leaves. Young leaves of many plants may contain higher levels of tannins and other polyphenolic compounds. However, their quantity and composition depend on the plant species, leaf age, season, and growing conditions.
  • 2. The presence of edible fruits. This is a practical guideline when selecting plants for preparing infusions. Examples include apple, strawberry, cherry, black chokeberry (aronia), raspberry, blackcurrant, pear, plum, blackthorn, cornelian cherry, oleaster, and grapevine. Willow leaves may also be considered for processing.

The fact that a plant produces edible fruits does not automatically mean that its leaves are suitable for consumption. The specific characteristics of each plant must be taken into account.

Main Processing Stages

  • 1. Leaf harvesting.
  • 2. Withering.
  • 3. Freezing (optional).
  • 4. Leaf preparation.
  • 5. Fermentation.
  • 6. Drying.
  • 7. Storage.

1. Leaf Harvesting

Leaves should be collected in dry weather. Plants growing in the shade often have juicier leaves. Whenever possible, avoid collecting raw material near busy roads, major industrial facilities, and pipelines.

Where possible, collect clean leaves and do not wash them. If the raw material is contaminated, it can be rinsed with clean water, after which excess surface moisture should be removed.

Leaves can be collected throughout the growing season:

  • Spring: young and tender leaves are easier to process and ferment.
  • Fruit-bearing period: leaves, like fruits, may accumulate various flavour and aromatic compounds.
  • Autumn: leaves become tougher, more difficult to process, and take longer to ferment. The amount of suitable material is usually lower due to damage caused by garden pests and plant diseases.

2. Withering

a) Indoor withering

Spread the leaves on a cotton or linen cloth in a layer 3–5 cm thick. Turn them periodically to ensure even withering.

Avoid direct sunlight. Withering outdoors may complicate processing: sunlight and wind can dry the leaves too quickly and reduce the quality of the final product.

  • Signs that withering is complete: loss of turgor (the leaves bend and curl rather than break), accompanied by a change in colour.
  • Time: approximately 12 hours on average, depending on the leaf species, temperature, air movement, and humidity.
  • Temperature: approximately 20–24 °C.
  • Relative humidity: approximately 70%.
  • Important notes:
    • The process is faster on dry, sunny days. In rainy weather, it may take 24 hours or longer.
    • Withered leaves roll more easily and are easier to process further.
    • Approximate residual moisture content of the leaves: 60–62%.

The end of the process can be assessed as follows: fold a leaf in half. If the central vein makes a cracking sound, withering should continue. When a handful of leaves is squeezed firmly, the resulting clump should retain its shape and not spring apart. The temperature, humidity, and residual moisture values given above are practical guidelines rather than strict technological standards. The actual duration of withering depends on the initial condition of the raw material and the surrounding environment.

b) Withering in cloth

If the indoor environment is too humid or too dry, cotton or linen cloth can be used to accelerate the process and make it more uniform. A dense or thick fabric is preferable.

Spread the leaves evenly in a thin layer over the cloth. Fold the fabric and roll it as tightly as possible. Leaves withered in this way produce firmer granules. They are also easier to process in a food processor. This method is suitable for tougher leaves.

3. Freezing

Freezing softens the leaves and makes subsequent processing easier. During freezing, ice crystals form and damage the leaf structure, helping to release cellular juice during subsequent processing.

Freezing is not an essential stage. Leaves can be used immediately after withering or stored frozen for no longer than two months.

4. Leaf Preparation

At this stage, the leaf structure must be disrupted to release cellular juice and bring enzymes into contact with the substances contained in the raw material. There are several ways to prepare the leaves.

a) Hand rolling

Roll 7–10 leaves firmly between the palms until they darken due to the release of cellular juice. This produces rolls approximately 10 cm long and 1–1.5 cm thick. The rolls can then be cut into smaller pieces. Depending on how finely they are cut, the result will be fine-cut or medium-cut leaf material.

b) Processing in a food processor

Pass the withered leaves through a food processor fitted with a coarse grinding plate. The result is coarse-cut leaf material.

c) Kneading (crushing the leaves)

Knead the leaves vigorously by hand in a deep, wide bowl for 10–15 minutes. During kneading, periodically loosen the clumps and separate leaves that have stuck together. This process disrupts the leaf structure and releases cellular juice. The result is coarse-cut leaf material.

5. Fermentation

Fermentation is the biochemical transformation of organic substances. In the process described here, the main role is played by the enzymatic oxidation of substances contained in the plant material. The enzymes may be naturally present in the leaves themselves.

Processing conditions:

  • Rolled leaves are placed in a container and pressed under a weight.
  • Kneaded leaves are placed in a container and pressed under a weight.
  • Leaves processed in a food processor are placed in a container and compressed by hand.

The container is then covered with a linen or cotton cloth and placed in a warm location. During the process, ensure that the raw material does not dry out. If necessary, periodically moisten the covering cloth. In a dry room, the container covered with cloth can also be partially covered with a lid, leaving a gap for air exchange.

Temperature guidelines:

The recommended temperature for the process is 22–26 °C.

  • Below 15 °C, enzymatic oxidation slows down.
  • Above 30 °C, the nature of the oxidation processes and the development of the final product's aromatic properties may change.

The process takes approximately 6–8 hours on average. Its duration and conditions depend on the plant species, the degree of preliminary leaf processing, the volume of raw material, and the surrounding environment.

The strongest and most intense aroma is an indication that the process is approaching its endpoint. After reaching its peak, the aroma begins to lose intensity and richness.

6. Drying

Kneaded or rolled leaves can be cut if necessary. Cutting them into pieces approximately 0.5 cm in size produces medium-cut material; finer cutting produces fine-cut material. If the leaves are not cut, the result is coarse-cut material, which occupies more storage space. Leaves processed in a food processor become granulated material.

Initial drying

Spread the raw material in a layer approximately 1 cm thick on a baking tray lined with parchment paper. Dry in an oven at 100 °C for 1–1.5 hours. Then reduce the oven temperature to 50–60 °C to remove the remaining moisture. Stir the material periodically to ensure even drying. Over-dried leaf material may lose its flavour and aroma. Brittleness can serve as an indication that the main drying stage is complete.

Natural finishing drying

To reduce residual moisture and bring the product to a condition suitable for long-term storage, additional drying can be carried out using natural air circulation. Transfer the material into bags made of thin cotton or linen fabric and place them in a well-ventilated area. This stage may take more than a week. Periodically inspect the material to prevent reabsorption of moisture and the development of mould.

Signs of readiness:

  • Well-dried granules do not crumble or get crushed easily; instead, they break apart.
  • When stirred, the material produces a dry, rustling sound.

Organoleptic indicators help assess readiness but do not provide an accurate measurement of residual moisture.

Experimental hypothesis

Some Chinese producers begin the drying process by raising the temperature to 125–150 °C for 10–20 minutes. It is hypothesised that this temperature promotes the thermal transformation of sugars and the development of additional aromatic notes.

The explanation of this effect in terms of caramelisation of plant sugars on the surface of the granules and the formation of a kind of coating that retains the remaining juice inside requires separate experimental verification. Therefore, this observation should be regarded as a technological hypothesis rather than an established physicochemical mechanism.

7. Storage

The finished product should be stored in plastic or glass containers with lids, in a dark and dry place. Before placing the product into long-term storage, make sure that the material is sufficiently dry and shows no signs of mould or unusual odours.

The flavour and aroma of the product change during storage. For the first use, the product is considered most suitable after one month of storage. During this period, its flavour and aromatic properties continue to develop and stabilise.
